\section{Can language models play?}\label{sec:can-they-play}

Every model can play the game. Across the \NumGames{} games, every game finished, and no seat ever lost a turn to three refusals in a row. Five DeepSeek games stopped on provider errors, and some games in two decision-seat lineups stopped when our OpenRouter key hit its spending limit. All of them were replayed from the start. Those stops came from the API, not from play.

\subsection{Following the rules}

The engine refuses a move that breaks a rule, and the model tries again. Most refusals were tool calls the engine could not use: a reply with no tool call, or a call to a move that wasn't legal at that moment. Table~\ref{tab:can-they-play} counts them per game. Sol made \SolRefusals{} in 360 games, and Terra made \TerraRefusals{}. Qwen 3.8 Flash made the most, at \QwenRefusalsPerGame{} a game. Some of Ember's refusals were calls that ran out of output tokens mid-thought and returned no move (Section~\ref{sec:cost}). The hybrids and the decision seats made none, since their moves come from a fixed list of options.

Every language model also used the special wedges. Each one flipped Mystery wedges, rode the Express and spent the Wild Card. The Luna Decisions seat never rode the Express. It passed all 10 times it could have.

\subsection{Reading the board}

A wrong solve is legal, but its kind shows how a model reads the board. A wrong answer that fits the board is a fair guess. The other kinds break something the board already shows. They put a called letter into a blank, contradict a letter on the board, repeat an answer already ruled wrong, or have the wrong number or length of words.

The models differ most in how they read. Luna made \LunaWrongAll{} wrong solves, and \LunaWrongBoard{} of them (\LunaWrongBoardShare) broke the board, most often by putting a letter already called into a blank. The Luna Decisions seat, whose solves Luna writes, did the same. Sol and Ember rarely broke the board: \SolWrongBoard{} of Sol's \SolWrongAll{} wrong solves did, and \EmberWrongBoard{} of Ember's \EmberWrongAll{}. Inside a hybrid, no wrong solve broke the board, because the bot only accepts a model's answer if it fits.

So the rules are not what separates the models. Each one plays legally on almost every move. What separates them is whether they use everything the board already shows.
